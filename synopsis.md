# Offline Multimodal RAG System for SIH 2025

## 1. INTRODUCTION

The growing reliance on digital information in education, administration, research, healthcare, public service, and enterprise environments has transformed the nature of knowledge access. Information is no longer confined to plain text documents or structured databases; instead, it is dispersed across PDFs, handwritten notes, scanned forms, screenshots, photographs, presentation slides, audio recordings, and video-based lectures. This diversity introduces an important challenge: the same underlying concept may be represented across multiple modalities, each with different semantics, structure, and retrieval patterns. As a result, traditional information retrieval systems that depend heavily on keyword matching or text-only indexing often fail to capture the full meaning of an information repository. The need for systems that can integrate and reason across multiple data types is therefore becoming more urgent.

This project addresses that challenge through the development of an Offline Multimodal Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system capable of ingesting, indexing, and querying heterogeneous data in a unified framework. The system is designed to process textual documents, visual content, and audio transcripts as different but interconnected sources of knowledge. It uses modality-specific extraction and representation techniques to convert each data type into a searchable semantic form and then retrieves relevant information through embedding-based similarity search. Once the evidence is identified, the system synthesizes a final answer using a local language model, ensuring that the response is not merely generated from memory but grounded in the sources the user has provided.

The broader importance of this project lies in the fact that knowledge work increasingly takes place in environments where cloud access is limited, privacy is essential, and computational resources are constrained. In such settings, offline intelligence is not a convenience but a necessity. University students may need to query lecture recordings and scattered notes without internet access; public agencies may process archival materials with strict confidentiality requirements; healthcare departments may need to retrieve scanned reports and audio notes locally; and small organizations may wish to deploy AI assistants without relying on external APIs. This system responds directly to those needs by providing a local, privacy-preserving multimodal knowledge assistant that can operate efficiently on consumer-grade hardware.

In one paragraph, the project can be described as an end-to-end architecture that transforms raw multimodal inputs into a searchable knowledge base and then uses retrieval-augmented reasoning to answer user questions with source-grounded evidence. Unlike text-only architecture or cloud-dependent support tools, it is designed to work offline, preserve user privacy, support cross-modal retrieval, and provide grounded outputs in a way that is practically deployable in real-world environments. Its contribution is therefore both technical and societal: it demonstrates how locally deployed multimodal AI can make knowledge access more accessible, more secure, and more context-aware.

## 2. NEED OF THE PROJECT

The need for this project emerges from a clear mismatch between the complexity of contemporary information ecosystems and the limitations of conventional search systems. In many real-world contexts, users work not only with documents but also with visual artifacts, scanned images, voice recordings, and extracted text from screenshots or printed pages. For example, a user may need to know whether a particular point mentioned in a lecture recording corresponds to content in a PDF handout or a photograph of a whiteboard. A classic search system based on keywords or textual metadata will fail to connect these different evidence sources effectively. Consequently, meaningful information remains fragmented across modalities and is not retrieved in a semantically coherent way.

This issue is particularly severe in settings where users are required to work offline or under strict data privacy constraints. In academic institutions, government offices, field operations, regional organizations, and low-resource industrial environments, internet access may be intermittent or prohibited, and the sharing of internal data with third-party cloud services may be unacceptable. The availability of local AI systems would therefore provide a safer and more realistic alternative, but such systems remain scarce. Most available tools are designed for generalized online use and either fail to support multimodal retrieval or require heavy computational and financial infrastructure.

The beneficiaries of this project are therefore broad and diverse. Students and researchers can use it to search lecture recordings, papers, and visual notes simultaneously. Public-sector employees can process tax forms, government documents, and field inspection photographs without depending on external APIs. Legal and medical professionals can retrieve text from scanned records and related audio or visual evidence in a single workflow. Even small businesses and independent organizations can benefit from local AI assistants that allow them to query internal documentation without exposing sensitive data to external providers. The project is especially valuable in hardware-constrained environments, where the goal is not simply maximum model size but effective local functionality under limited resources.

Existing solutions are insufficient for several reasons. Many are designed for a single modality, such as text-only retrieval or image captioning alone. Others require continuous cloud connectivity, high-end infrastructure, or proprietary payment plans that are unsuitable for local deployments. Some enterprises and institutions also need a system that can cite the origin of each answer to ensure transparency and accountability. This project addresses those limitations by combining local retrieval, multimodal indexing, and grounded answer generation in a single architecture. It is therefore motivated by both technical necessity and practical impact: the system fills a real gap in the market for privacy-aware, offline, multimodal knowledge assistance.

## 3. PROBLEM STATEMENT

The problem addressed by this project is the lack of an integrated, offline, multimodal information retrieval and reasoning system capable of retrieving evidence across text, image, and audio sources in a unified and grounded manner. Most existing retrieval systems are optimized for single-modality inputs and therefore fail to effectively handle semantically related content distributed across documents, images, screenshots, audio recordings, and transcribed conversations. This fragmentation weakens user productivity and reduces the quality of decision making when knowledge is stored in heterogeneous formats.

Additionally, many practical AI systems depend on cloud infrastructure, which introduces constraints related to connectivity, privacy, cost, latency, and compliance. In environments where data sensitivity matters and internet access is unreliable or prohibited, these dependencies render the systems unusable. This project formalizes the problem as designing a local multimodal RAG architecture that can convert heterogeneous sources into searchable embeddings, retrieve the most relevant evidence, and generate responses backed by source citations. The solution must operate under constraints such as limited hardware capability, offline deployment, moderate model sizes, and the need for scalable indexing across diverse data types.

## 4. HARDWARE AND SOFTWARE REQUIREMENTS

### 4.1 Hardware Requirements

A practical implementation of this project requires a machine capable of running local inference and indexing without excessive latency. The minimum recommended configuration includes an NVIDIA GPU with at least 6 GB VRAM, although 8–12 GB VRAM is preferred for smoother handling of multimodal extraction, embedding generation, and local LLM inference. The GPU is particularly important for accelerating model execution and processing larger document batches or image-heavy datasets. A CPU such as an Intel Core i5/i7 or AMD Ryzen 5/7 is also suitable for general orchestration, while a multi-core processor improves performance when parsing documents or processing queued retrieval tasks.

Memory requirements depend on the scale of the dataset and the number of models loaded into memory simultaneously. A minimum of 16 GB RAM is required for basic operation, but 32 GB RAM is recommended for more robust usage involving document indexing, vector storage, intermediate processing, and full local model execution. Storage must also be considered seriously. A system with at least 50 GB of free SSD space is recommended, while 100 GB or more is preferable in environments handling large corpora, multiple models, or persistent data stores. Optional hardware such as a microphone may be useful for voice-based search support, while stable local storage is essential for maintaining the vector database and source metadata.

### 4.2 Software Requirements

The software stack is selected to prioritize offline capability, open-source availability, and practical deployment. The project is designed to work on Windows 10/11 or Linux-based systems, with Python 3.10 or later serving as the primary programming environment. For backend services, the architecture uses FastAPI, which provides a lightweight and efficient API layer for communication between the frontend and the retrieval pipeline. The frontend is implemented using React, TypeScript, and Vite to create a responsive interface for query submission, source display, and session management.

The system integrates several specialized tools for multimodal processing. Ollama provides local model hosting for language generation, allowing the system to run without cloud APIs. Embedding generation relies on BGE or sentence-transformer-based encoders for text semantics, while CLIP-based architectures support cross-modal alignment between text and visual data. Image processing is enhanced through BLIP for caption generation and Tesseract OCR for text extraction from screenshots and scanned documents. Audio processing uses faster-whisper for speech-to-text transcription and timestamped retrieval. Document parsing is handled using PyMuPDF and python-docx, while ChromaDB acts as the local vector store for persistent indexing and semantic search. Supporting libraries include PyTorch, Transformers, OpenCV, Pillow, and Hugging Face model dependencies. Together, these tools create a modular but integrated pipeline suitable for local, privacy-aware multimodal AI systems.

## 5. LITERATURE REVIEW

### 5a. Funnel Structure

The general problem addressed by this project is the difficulty of effectively retrieving and reasoning over large volumes of heterogeneous knowledge in real-world digital environments. Organizations and users increasingly interact with content that is not stored in a single format; instead, knowledge exists across documents, images, scanned reports, screenshots, audio recordings, and video-based explanations. This multimodal distribution of information makes conventional text-based retrieval systems increasingly insufficient. Much of the value in a corpus lies not in an isolated document or an audio clip, but in the relationships among them. Therefore, a high-quality information system must be capable of identifying the semantic connections between textual, visual, and auditory evidence and using them to answer user queries in context.

The specific focus of this study is narrower but more operationally significant: the design of an offline multimodal RAG system that can process documents, images, and audio in a unified retrieval pipeline and generate source-grounded responses. At this level, the system is not only expected to extract meaningful content from each modality, but also to align those representations within a common latent space so that relevant evidence can be retrieved regardless of source type. This challenge sits at the intersection of information retrieval, computer vision, speech processing, transformer-based modeling, and retrieval-augmented generation. It is also directly relevant to low-resource and privacy-aware deployment, where local AI systems must be functional without internet connectivity or external data processing.

The literature in this space reveals several important strands of development. Early and foundational work in retrieval-augmented generation introduced a powerful architecture in which external evidence is retrieved and used to condition generation, leading to more factual and grounded outputs (Lewis et al., 2020). In parallel, vision-language models began to show that visual and textual representations can be aligned semantically, enabling zero-shot retrieval and multimodal reasoning (Radford et al., 2021). Speech recognition systems also evolved to a point where multimodal repositories could include spoken content as structured textual evidence (Radford et al., 2022). Dense retrieval methods further improved search quality by using learned embeddings rather than lexical matching alone (Karpukhin et al., 2020). While these developments are substantial, they still usually solve sub-problems rather than the full integrated challenge of multimodal, offline, context-aware retrieval.

A major limitation of current solutions is that they are often built around individual modalities or require cloud infrastructure for production deployment. Vision-language models are strong for image-text understanding, but their computational burden makes local applications difficult without careful optimization. Speech-to-text and OCR pipelines are useful but frequently suffer from noise, context loss, or incomplete extraction when they are applied to real-world unstructured files. Dense retrieval improves relevant result ranking, yet it does not inherently solve the challenge of unifying multiple modalities into a single retrieval layer. This leaves a clear methodological gap: the field has not yet produced a widely deployable architecture that combines text, image, and audio retrieval into a single offline system with grounded generation and source attribution. This motivates the research question: how can a local multimodal RAG system integrate extraction, representation, retrieval, and generation across heterogeneous sources while remaining feasible on constrained hardware and preserving user privacy? The aim of this study is to propose and evaluate such a system in a practically relevant setting.

### 5b. Concentric Circle Approach

The relevant literature may be organized in concentric circles, beginning with work that is most directly aligned with the project and progressively widening toward more general foundations. At the center of this structure is the problem of offline multimodal retrieval and grounded query answering across multiple data sources. The central methodological anchor is retrieval-augmented generation, as formalized by Lewis et al. (2020). Their work demonstrated that combining a retriever with a generator can significantly improve factual consistency and contextual grounding. This is highly relevant because the present project does not generate an answer from memory alone. Instead, it retrieves the most relevant evidence from a local multimodal corpus and then uses that evidence to shape the final answer. This is conceptually aligned with RAG and extends it to heterogeneous sources rather than text-only corpora.

The second ring contains the closely related multimodal frameworks that most directly support the present system. Radford et al. (2021) introduced CLIP, a contrastive learning model that aligns images and text within a shared embedding space. This is foundational to cross-modal retrieval because it enables semantic similarity between visual and textual representations. The project benefits directly from this property because an image may be used as a query to retrieve related textual content, or a textual query may retrieve matching visual evidence. Li et al. (2022) introduced BLIP, a vision-language pretraining framework designed for understanding and generation tasks. BLIP is particularly relevant because it shows how visual scene understanding can be integrated with language to support multimodal semantics. Liu et al. (2023), through LLaVA, demonstrated an instruction-tuned multimodal model capable of interpreting images with language guidance, highlighting the importance of combining visual and textual reasoning for natural interaction. Together, these studies show that multimodal reasoning is not only theoretically sound but practically enforceable in modern AI systems.

The third ring comprises the underlying technologies most directly used in the present implementation: dense retrieval, local large language models, speech transcription, and OCR. Karpukhin et al. (2020) established the importance of dense retrieval, showing that neural embedding-based retrieval often outperforms sparse lexical retrieval. This is significant because the system creates an embedding-based search space for text, image captions, and transcript chunks. In the local language generation layer, open models such as Qwen (Bai et al., 2023) and smaller instruction-tuned systems such as Phi-3 (Abdin et al., 2024) illustrate how modern AI models can be adapted for low-resource deployment. Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) is especially relevant because it allows audio content to be converted into machine-readable text, facilitating retrieval from spoken material. OCR methods and visual-language models also enable image-derived text to be extracted and converted into a structured form for semantic search. These technologies collectively enable the system to translate raw, unstructured modalities into evidence units that can be indexed and cited.

The fourth ring is composed of the broader technical foundations that support the project’s architecture, especially vector databases, transformer encoders, and retrieval pipelines. Although not all of these works are strictly multimodal, they provide the computational and design principles that make multimodal RAG viable. Transformers provide the representational power needed to encode text and image features into semantically meaningful vectors; vector databases allow efficient similarity search across large corpora; and retrieval pipelines make it possible to rank candidate evidence before language generation occurs. These components are central to the project because they allow different types of content to be normalized into a shared retrieval space. In a multimodal system, this standardization is essential: a user should not need to know whether the answer was derived from a PDF paragraph, a screenshot caption, or a transcript segment. The retrieval layer should abstract those differences while preserving provenance.

The outermost ring includes broader research on offline AI, edge deployment, and privacy-preserving computation. While this body of literature is not focused solely on multimodal retrieval, it is highly relevant to the practical motivations of the project. Research on local and edge AI emphasizes the value of running inference without transferring data to remote servers, particularly when privacy, latency, or connectivity constraints are significant. In sectors such as public administration, healthcare, education, and field operations, local deployment is often a decisive requirement rather than an optional enhancement. This wider literature therefore helps situate the present project in a meaningful context: it is not merely an academic exercise in model integration, but a response to the need for trustworthy local intelligence in the real world.

## 7. METHODOLOGY

The methodological framework of this project follows a modular but integrated pipeline that begins with data ingestion and ends with grounded answer generation. The system operates by first collecting heterogeneous user inputs, which may include PDFs, DOCX files, scanned images, screenshots, voice notes, and audio clips. These inputs are then passed through modality-specific processing stages. Document files are parsed to extract text while preserving metadata such as page numbers, file names, and document structure. Visual content is processed using OCR to detect embedded text and captioning or vision-language models to understand scene semantics. Audio inputs are transcribed into textual segments using speech recognition models, enabling spoken content to be indexed and retrieved as textual evidence.

Once extracted, the content is transformed into embeddings using sentence-transformers or comparable text encoders, while image-text alignment models such as CLIP provide cross-modal matching where relevant. These embeddings are then stored in a local vector database such as ChromaDB, which supports efficient similarity search and retrieval. Each indexed item is associated with source metadata and chunk-level provenance so that the system can cite the exact origin of a retrieved fact. This is a crucial feature because the project is not intended to function as a black-box answer generator; rather, it is designed to operate as a retrieval-grounded assistant whose outputs can be verified against the underlying corpus.

The retrieval stage follows a hybrid reasoning process. When a user submits a query, the system encodes the query into the same semantic space as the stored embeddings and retrieves the most relevant chunks based on similarity scores. This is performed over document segments, image captions, transcript text, and possibly visual embeddings depending on the query type. If the query is textual, the system can retrieve from all modalities. If the query is image-based, it may use visual-language alignment to locate related textual or visual evidence. If the query is audio-based, the system may transcribe and compare spoken content with indexed corpus segments. The retrieval process therefore supports cross-modal inferencing rather than pure same-modality search.

After retrieval, the project applies a generation step in which the user question and the retrieved evidence are combined in a prompt presented to a local LLM. The model is instructed to generate a direct answer using the evidence, while preserving factual consistency and avoiding unsupported claims. To ensure grounded output, the prompt and the retrieval logic may explicitly constrain the model to rely on the retrieved excerpts and to reference the source metadata when explaining the answer. This generation step is critical because it bridges the gap between raw retrieval and usable intelligence. It enables the system to synthesize responses that are not only relevant but also traceable to the original data.

The methodological design also integrates practical engineering considerations. Since the project emphasizes local deployment, it is important to implement model initialization, caching, and retrieval workflows in a way that avoids unnecessary overhead. This includes reusing embedding models, avoiding repeated re-indexing of unchanged data, and using lightweight chunking and metadata strategies that balance retrieval quality with speed. The system is structured so that it can be deployed on modest hardware while still maintaining a useful user experience. The methodological emphasis is therefore not just on research novelty, but on the construction of a robust system that can be used in real settings without requiring cloud resources or high-end infrastructure.

## 9. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE AND FUNCTIONAL WORKFLOW

The architectural design of the proposed system is intended to mirror a realistic information-processing pipeline, beginning with raw heterogeneous data and ending with grounded user interaction. At a high level, the architecture contains four major layers: input acquisition, modality-specific preprocessing, semantic indexing and retrieval, and answer generation. Each layer is built to address a distinct challenge while preserving integration across the full system. This modularity is not merely a software design preference; it is essential for a system working across diverse data types and operating constraints.

The input acquisition layer is responsible for accepting raw files and user queries from the frontend interface. Users may upload documents, images, and audio files, or they may ask a question directly using text or voice input. The system interacts with a local backend service that receives the uploaded artifacts, inspects their file types, and forwards them to the appropriate extraction processes. This stage also manages metadata such as file name, timestamp, storage path, and indexing session information. Because the project is intended for offline operation, these files remain on the local device, and no external API call is required for their processing.

The second layer, modality-specific preprocessing, focuses on converting each input into machine-readable and semantically meaningful content. Document files are processed using parsers that preserve structure such as headings, paragraphs, tables, and page numbers. This is important because a query may ask for a specific fact that appears in a scanned report or a selected section of a PDF. Visual inputs, such as screenshots or images containing text, are processed by OCR and visual-language models to identify embedded text, scene structure, and contextual descriptions. Audio files are transcribed using multilingual speech recognition methods and then segmented into manageable units that can later be matched to user queries. This layer is therefore critical for turning noisy and heterogeneous multimodal inputs into unified evidence units.

The third layer is the retrieval and indexing layer, where extracted content is encoded and stored in a vector space. Text chunks, image captions, transcribed segments, and related metadata are transformed into dense embeddings using pretrained semantic encoders. These embeddings are stored in a local vector database that supports high-speed similarity matching and hierarchical retrieval. The design allows the system to perform both direct and cross-modal retrieval. For example, a user may ask a question in natural language and retrieve relevant document passages; similarly, an image input may be matched against captions of previously indexed images or textual descriptions of associated documents. This capability distinguishes the project from a simple keyword system and makes it relevant in realistic multimodal knowledge environments.

The final layer is the answer generation and justification layer. Once the relevant evidence segments are retrieved, the system constructs a prompt that includes the user query and the most relevant fetched content. The local large language model then produces a response grounded in this evidence. This is the point at which the system demonstrates the advantage of retrieval-augmented generation: the model is not left to rely solely on its internal memory, but is anchored in the actual sources contained in the local corpus. The answer is then presented with citations or source references, allowing the user to trace the evidence back to the corresponding document, page, segment, or image metadata. This layer is crucial for trust, usability, and reproducibility, especially in settings involving institutional records or academic material.

The architecture is therefore designed to be practical and modular instead of monolithic. Each stage can be replaced or upgraded independently without destabilizing the entire system. For example, a more advanced OCR model can be incorporated without redesigning the entire retrieval pipeline, and a different local LLM can be used for generation without changing the vector database or indexing layer. This flexibility is important because model quality, deployment constraints, and user needs vary across environments. In academic and industrial terms, the architecture demonstrates a robust design philosophy: combine specialized component performance with a common semantic retrieval layer and strong evidence grounding.

## 10. LIMITATIONS, CHALLENGES, AND FUTURE SCOPE

Although the project is strategically well aligned with the needs of multimodal offline AI, it is not without limitations. One major challenge is the performance-cost tradeoff associated with local inference. Large language models and vision-language models can provide stronger reasoning quality, but their computational requirements may be burdensome for machines with limited GPU memory or modest CPU capabilities. For this reason, the project must balance model scale with practical usability. Smaller models may run faster and more reliably on local hardware, but they may produce weaker reasoning or less accurate retrieval under complex prompts. This tradeoff is a central constraint in the design process and a fundamental reality of low-resource AI deployment.

Another challenge is noise in real-world multimodal data. OCR can misread scanned or distorted text, image captioning may fail to capture the relevant context in a crowded scene, and transcription may introduce errors when a spoken file contains background noise, dialect variation, or multiple speakers. These errors can degrade retrieval quality or mislead the answer generation stage if not handled carefully. The project mitigates this issue through chunking, metadata preservation, and evidence grounding, but it cannot eliminate it entirely. This is a realistic limitation in any system that operates on heterogeneous, imperfect real-world data.

In addition, the system’s performance depends on the quality of the retrieval and chunking strategies. If the corpus is poorly segmented or if embeddings are weakly aligned with the query distribution, the system may retrieve irrelevant or partial evidence. This makes the design sensitive to chunk size, embedding choice, and metadata quality. It also means that some tasks may require fine-tuning or domain-specific adaptation to achieve better results. This limitation is common in retrieval-powered systems, but it remains important from both research and usability perspectives.

The future scope of the project is considerable. One direction is to extend the system to support more modalities, such as video and tables, so that temporal and structured content can be indexed more effectively. Another is to incorporate hybrid retrieval strategies that combine dense embeddings with sparse lexical matching, improving performance on technical or domain-specific documents. The system could also be extended with user-specific personalization, session memory, and richer metadata extraction for improved context retention. Additionally, future versions could support multi-user deployment or local enterprise search scenarios in which several datasets are managed within the same knowledge layer.

A further promising direction is the application of domain-specific adaptation. For example, in legal or medical environments, the system could be extended with specialized document understanding models and ontology-aware retrieval. In educational settings, it could support lecture summarization, question-answering, and adaptive tutoring across multimodal content. These future enhancements would build directly on the current architecture while broadening its real-world value. In this sense, the current project should be seen as a foundational system that opens opportunities for more specialized and advanced multimodal knowledge assistants.

This synopsis presents the project as a practical and academically grounded response to a real-world challenge: the fragmented and multimodal nature of modern information. The proposed system is designed to provide a structured, privacy-aware, and locally deployable solution for intelligent knowledge retrieval and reasoning across documents, images, and audio. By integrating the strongest ideas from multimodal learning, dense retrieval, and retrieval-augmented generation, it offers a coherent and technically defensible framework for future development. The project is therefore not only relevant to SIH 2025 but also aligned with current research directions in multimodal AI, trustworthy generation, and offline intelligence.
